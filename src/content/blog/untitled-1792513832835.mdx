This story begins in 2023-24 and has 4 components:

1. I love static websites;
2. I needed to update my portfolio;
3. I worked with Sahil Lavingia;
4. I worked for startups.

## Static websites

This is an easy one. How could you not love them? It's such an elegant medium. It works fast, looks great, simple as hell. Oftentimes looks great. Look at [this](https://nat.org/) website, for example.

## Portfolio

My old portfolio looked like Nat Friedman's website, which doesn't work really well unless you invested in GitHub. And it was made on WordPress, which I never really liked. Their CEO Matt already did some madd shit, and this didn't look cool to me.

I went to check what other people use for their personal websites and found [Astro.js](https://astro.build/). I really liked the concept. I was especially sold when I discovered their view transitions.

Also, Astro uses components. It's a close-to-my-heart topic, as I was obsessed with modular systems earlier in my life. I thought to myself: why hasn't anybody built a drag-and-drop editor for that? It's so obviously low-hanging fruit. Non-technical people like SEOs and marketers would love it. But I really had no idea how to build it. I finished coding my website and deployed it.

## Working with Sahil

It was a wild roller coaster. Gumroad was hiring. I sent Sahil my portfolio and did a test task, and he hired me. I didn't stay for too long. But what I saw there was a confirmation of what I was hoping existed somewhere in the world.

Everyone was writing code in one form or another. Support people would fix bugs themselves using AI. Sahil, the CEO, would write code too. Back then, Cursor had only just appeared. Oftentimes people would copy some code into Claude chat, and copy the fixed code back into the file.

They were insanely effective at work and everyone made crazy money. Sahil told me, "Money grows on trees, time doesn't," and then I had to say goodbye to Gumroad.

## Startup websites

I rarely built marketing websites and would only see every now and then how my marketing colleagues were struggling with Webflow and how they were praising Framer. Until one day I was helping [Netmaker](https://www.netmaker.io/) and they asked me to update their hero section. They were using Webflow.

I really struggle to find the words to describe my disappointment with this product, after dealing with websites in HTML and CSS. The cherry on top was when Netmaker wanted to enable the autopilot feature and Webflow's salesperson asked for $50,000 a year to activate it.

---

After work I went for a walk with my dog and was thinking about how something elegant like an HTML and CSS static website could be so violated by the wrong UI metaphor and implemented with zero sensuality, and then, to use it together, you would need to pay $50k.

This really didn't make any sense to me. Luckily, not long ago I also was at Gumroad, so I still remembered their psychopathic grind attitude. And I was like, fuck it, I'm building it. It's just a drag-and-drop editor for static components. I know components.

I told Perplexity what I wanted and it created some prototype. Then, when I got back home, I checked it: it was dropping 2 components into a canvas and exporting an HTML file. I sent it to my Homie and said "hey dude, easy money idea, pls add Astro to this prototype". And we started to develop it.

We developed the first version quite quickly. Then I realized I wanted to edit text, then I realized it lacked responsive design, and then it quickly grew into a project of enormous size where by fixing one thing I'd break half of the app. But working with startups showed me that you can be an absolute idiot, and still build a product people love. So I kept grinding religiously every day after work.

Two years later, I'm writing this blog from the Starquix editor I built. It's not a promotional post, but rather dogfooding. I'm working on the deployment feature, so once I'm done I'm gonna deploy it right from here and check how it works. It wasn't easy money, though. There was just no money, but it was fun, and now I have my piece of the internet.
